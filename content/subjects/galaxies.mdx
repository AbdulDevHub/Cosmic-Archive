## An island of stars

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">A galaxy is made of stars, planets and vast clouds of gas and dust, all bound together by gravity.</Claim> The range is enormous. <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">The largest contain trillions of stars and can be more than a million light-years across. The smallest may hold a few thousand stars and span just a few hundred light-years.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Most large galaxies have a supermassive black hole at their centre, some with billions of times the Sun's mass.</Claim> Those have a page of their own, Black Holes.

For a long time ours seemed to be the only one. <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">In the early 1900s the Milky Way was viewed as a single "island universe" of stars. In 1923 Edwin Hubble observed a Cepheid variable star in Andromeda and proved it was a separate galaxy, far beyond our own.</Claim>

## Reading the picture

The galaxy at the top of this page is our own illustration of a spiral, and each labelled part stands for something real.

<Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">A spiral galaxy is like a giant rotating pinwheel: a flat, pancake-like disc of stars around a central bulge, a tight concentration of stars.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">The youngest stars form in the gas-rich arms, while older stars are found throughout the disc and within the bulge and the halo.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">The halo surrounds the whole thing: a mixture of old stars, star clusters and dark matter, an invisible material that gives off and reflects no light but still pulls on other matter with its gravity.</Claim> That is why the halo can only be hinted at in the picture.

One thing in the picture is simpler than the real Milky Way. <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Our galaxy and Andromeda are both barred spirals, with a ribbon of stars, gas and dust cutting across the centre. Barred spirals make up two-thirds of all spirals.</Claim> The galaxy drawn here has no bar.

## Shapes

<Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Scientists sometimes sort galaxies by their shapes and physical features.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Most are spirals or ellipticals; those with less orderly shapes are usually called irregular.</Claim>

<Diagram name="galaxy-types" source="nasa-galaxy-types">

1. **Spiral.** A flat disc and a central bulge, with arms that can be wound tightly or loosely. Seen edge on, the arms may not be visible at all.
2. **Barred spiral.** A spiral with a ribbon of stars, gas and dust across its centre. The Milky Way is one.
3. **Lenticular.** The bulge and disc of a spiral, but no arms, mostly old stars and little new star formation.
4. **Elliptical.** Round to oval, with little gas and dust and very little structure. Its stars orbit the core in random directions.
5. **Irregular.** Unusual shapes, from toothpicks and rings to loose groupings of stars.

</Diagram>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Elliptical galaxies are less common than spirals. Their stars are generally older, because little of the gas needed to form new ones remains.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Irregular galaxies range from dwarfs of 100 million times the Sun's mass to large ones of 10 billion.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Shape is not the only way to sort them. Around 10% of known galaxies are active: their centres appear more than 100 times brighter than the combined light of their stars.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">The most luminous active galaxies are quasars.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">The Milky Way is not active at the moment, although it probably had a burst of activity in the past few million years.</Claim>

<Evidence status="hypothetical">

How each shape came about is still argued over. <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Scientists think elliptical galaxies come from collisions and mergers with spirals.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Lenticulars may be older spirals whose arms have faded, or they may have formed from mergers of spirals.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Some scientists think large irregular galaxies could be an intermediate step between spirals and ellipticals.</Claim>

</Evidence>

## How galaxies form and grow

<Evidence status="theoretical">

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-galaxies">Computer models of galaxy formation indicate that galaxies are created when dark matter merges and clumps together.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-galaxies">Dark matter's total mass in the universe is roughly five times that of ordinary matter, the atoms we are made of. It can be thought of as the scaffolding of the universe, and visible matter collects inside it as stars and galaxies.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-galaxies">In these models small objects form first and are drawn together into larger ones.</Claim>

</Evidence>

That building up can be watched. <Claim source="nasa-imagine-galaxies">Galaxy assembly is still going on today: we see many galaxies colliding and merging to form new ones.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxy-types">Odd shapes are sometimes the result: a spiral passing another galaxy with a stronger pull can lose some of its material and be distorted into a new shape.</Claim>

Looking far away is looking back in time, because light takes time to travel. <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">Many of the most distant galaxies are small and clumpy, often dominated by massive knots of intense star formation, little like the orderly spirals and ellipticals around us now.</Claim> <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">The Webb telescope has found galaxies that existed less than 300 million years after the Big Bang. In June 2025 a galaxy called MoM-z14 was confirmed as existing about 280 million years after the Big Bang, which took the distance record.</Claim>

Galaxies are old. <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Most are between 10 billion and 13.6 billion years old, and some are almost as old as the universe, which formed around 13.8 billion years ago. The youngest known galaxy is thought to have formed about 500 million years ago.</Claim>

Even the familiar flat disc took time to settle. <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">From 111 edge-on disc galaxies of different ages, Webb data suggest that a thick disc of stars forms first, from turbulent gas, and a thin disc forms inside it later, once the gas calms.</Claim>

## Scale and our place

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Our home galaxy, the Milky Way, is a spiral with a disc of stars more than 100,000 light-years across. Earth is on one of its spiral arms, about halfway from the centre.</Claim> <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">We are about 26,000 light-years from the centre.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">The solar system takes about 240 million years to go once around.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">From Earth the Milky Way is a faint, milky band of light arching across the sky, which is how it got its name. The band is the central disc of our galaxy, seen edge on.</Claim>

No galaxy is alone. <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">The Milky Way sits in a neighbourhood of more than 50 galaxies called the Local Group, from dwarf galaxies of up to a few billion stars to Andromeda, our nearest large neighbour.</Claim> <Claim source="esa-andromeda-fate">One of the Milky Way's satellite galaxies is the Large Magellanic Cloud; Andromeda has a large satellite of its own, M33.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Galaxies gather into groups of about 100 or fewer, held together by their mutual gravity. Clusters may hold thousands. Groups and clusters can be arranged in superclusters, which are not bound by gravity.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">The Local Group sits just off the edge of the Virgo cluster and is part of the Laniakea supercluster.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Superclusters, empty voids, walls of galaxies and other large structures make up the cosmic web of matter in the universe.</Claim> The atlas zooms out to that web.

## What telescopes show

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-deep-fields">In 1995 Hubble stared for ten days at a patch of sky about a thirteenth of the full Moon's width that looked almost empty. The Hubble Deep Field held roughly 3,000 distant galaxies.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-deep-fields">In 2004 the Hubble Ultra Deep Field, an exposure a million seconds long, held 10,000.</Claim>

<Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">Webb's JADES survey looked at and around the same area and saw more than 45,000 galaxies, some from when the universe was less than 600 million years old. That number is far beyond what was predicted before Webb launched.</Claim>

The early universe keeps surprising astronomers. <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">There are more bright early galaxies than expected: galaxies were forming stars earlier, and in greater numbers, than computer models suggested.</Claim> <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">Spiral structures like ours have been found earlier than expected, and one survey found that roughly 50 to 80% of the early galaxies it studied appear flattened in two dimensions.</Claim> <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">None of this has contradicted the best current models of the universe or its age.</Claim>

## What nobody knows yet

<Unknowns>

- **Why there are so many bright early galaxies.** <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">The cause is still uncertain and needs to be investigated.</Claim>
- **Where the early nitrogen came from.** <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">Webb found plenty of nitrogen in early galaxies. Nitrogen comes from aged low-mass stars, and at that time there had not been enough time for even one generation of them to grow old.</Claim>
- **How clumpy beginnings became spirals and ellipticals.** <Claim source="webb-galaxies-over-time">How the chaotic early galaxies turned into the orderly shapes we see today is one of the questions Webb is working on.</Claim>
- **How black holes and their galaxies shape each other.** <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">Supermassive black holes are thought to help regulate star formation in galaxies, but how that role developed over time is still being investigated.</Claim> <Claim source="webb-galaxies-through-time">Webb is also finding early black holes much more massive than expected for the size of their galaxies.</Claim>
- **Whether we will collide with Andromeda.** <Claim source="esa-andromeda-fate">A head-on collision once seemed almost certain. A 2025 study using Hubble and Gaia data found only about a 50-50 chance of the two galaxies colliding within the next 10 billion years, and about 2% for a head-on collision in 4 to 5 billion.</Claim> <Claim source="esa-andromeda-fate">Its authors call the fate of the Milky Way an open question.</Claim>

</Unknowns>
