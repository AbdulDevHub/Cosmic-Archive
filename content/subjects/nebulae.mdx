## A cloud between the stars

<Claim source="nasa-spaceplace-nebula">A nebula is a giant cloud of dust and gas in space, made mostly of hydrogen and helium.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-spaceplace-nebula">Nebulae lie in the space between the stars, which is called interstellar space.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-spaceplace-nebula">Some are regions where new stars are beginning to form, which is why some nebulae are called star nurseries. Others come from the gas and dust thrown out by the explosion of a dying star.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-stars">Stars form in clouds of gas and dust called molecular clouds, which range from 1,000 to 10 million times the mass of the Sun and can span as much as hundreds of light-years.</Claim>

## Reading the picture

The cloud at the top of this page is our own illustration of a place where stars are being born. Each labelled part stands for something seen in real ones.

At its centre is a young cluster in a hollow. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m42">The bright centre of the Orion Nebula is home to four massive young stars, the Trapezium, which were born together and now shape the nebula. Their ultraviolet light is carving a cavity in it.</Claim>

Around the hollow is a bright rim. <Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">At the edge of the star-forming region NGC 3324, which Webb pictured as the Cosmic Cliffs, the blistering ultraviolet radiation from young stars is sculpting the nebula's wall by slowly eroding it away.</Claim>

Columns of dust stand into the light. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-pillars-2015">Hubble's 1995 picture of the Pillars of Creation showed three giant columns of cold gas. Newborn stars are hidden inside them, and the bluish haze around their dense edges is material being heated up and evaporating into space.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m16">The pillars are bathed in ultraviolet light from a cluster of young stars just outside the frame, and the winds from those stars are slowly eroding the towers of gas and dust.</Claim>

The rest of the cloud glows. <Claim source="nasa-decoding-nebulae">Emission nebulae are bright, diffuse clouds of ionised gas that give off their own light.</Claim>

One thing in the picture is our choice rather than nature's: the colours. <Claim source="nasa-decoding-nebulae">In one optical image of the Eagle Nebula, blue represents oxygen, red is sulfur, and green signals both nitrogen and hydrogen.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m42">In a Hubble image of the Orion Nebula, orange is hydrogen, green is oxygen, and red is both sulfur and infrared light.</Claim> The rose, teal and gold drawn here are this site's palette, not a map of elements.

## Kinds of nebula

<Claim source="nasa-decoding-nebulae">Not all nebulae are alike, and how they look tells us what is happening around them.</Claim>

<Diagram name="nebula-kinds" source="nasa-decoding-nebulae, nasa-spaceplace-nebula">

1. **Emission.** Bright, diffuse clouds of ionised gas that shine with their own light. The reddish glow comes from excited atoms of gas.
2. **Reflection.** Dust that scatters the light of stars in or near it, a bit like a streetlamp lighting up fog. The dust reflects blue starlight best, so these clouds look bluish.
3. **Dark.** Dust so thick that the visible light of young stars cannot shine through it.
4. **Planetary nebula.** The glowing gas around a dying, Sun-like star.
5. **Supernova remnant.** The gas and dust thrown out when a dying star explodes.

</Diagram>

## Where stars are born

<Claim source="nasa-stars">Molecular clouds are cold, which makes the gas clump into high-density pockets. In time gravity makes some of these clumps collapse.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-spaceplace-nebula">The collapse heats the material at the centre of the clump, and this hot core is the beginning of a star.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-webb-pillars-2022">In Webb's image of the Pillars of Creation, the newly formed stars are estimated to be only a few hundred thousand years old.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-pillars-2022">Young stars periodically shoot out supersonic jets that collide with the clouds around them. The crimson glow in Webb's picture comes from energetic hydrogen molecules that result from those jets and shocks.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-pillars-2022">The pillars look like solid rock formations, but they are far more permeable.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">Star birth can spread over time: as the bright, ionised rim of an eroding cavity moves into the nebula, it pushes into the gas and dust, and where it meets unstable material the extra pressure can make that material collapse.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">The same disturbance can also stop star formation, as the material for new stars is eroded away. It is a very delicate balance between sparking star formation and stopping it.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m42">In Orion, the Trapezium's ultraviolet light is disrupting the growth of hundreds of smaller stars.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-pillars-2015">When Hubble returned to the Pillars in 2014, one of the astronomers said they had been caught "at a very unique and short-lived moment in their evolution".</Claim>

## Where stars leave their mark

<Claim source="nasa-stars">When a star like the Sun nears its end, all its outer layers blow away, making an expanding cloud of dust and gas called a planetary nebula. All that is left of the star is its core, a white dwarf: a roughly Earth-sized cinder that cools over billions of years.</Claim>

<Claim source="esahubble-planetary-nebula">Despite their name, planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets. The name is a historical mistake: 250 years ago, astronomers thought they were looking at gas planets.</Claim> <Claim source="esahubble-planetary-nebula">They last only about 20,000 years, a very short-lived part of a star's life.</Claim>

<Claim source="esawebb-ring-nebula">The Ring Nebula, roughly 2,500 light-years away, is an archetypal planetary nebula, formed by a star throwing off its outer layers as it ran out of fuel. It holds some 20,000 dense globules rich in molecular hydrogen.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-stars">A massive star ends in a huge explosion called a supernova, and its core survives as an incredibly dense remnant, either a neutron star or a black hole.</Claim> Black holes have a page of their own.

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-m1">In 1054, Chinese astronomers noticed a "guest star" that was visible in the daytime sky for nearly a month. It was a supernova, and it gave rise to the Crab Nebula, a remnant six light-years wide, 6,500 light-years away in the constellation Taurus.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m1">A rapidly spinning neutron star, the ultra-dense core of the exploded star, is embedded at its centre.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-stars">Material cast into space by supernovae and other stellar events enriches future molecular clouds and becomes part of the next generation of stars.</Claim>

## How far, how big

<Claim source="nasa-spaceplace-nebula">The closest known nebula to Earth is the Helix Nebula, approximately 700 light-years away.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m42">The Orion Nebula is only 1,500 light-years away, which makes it the closest large star-forming region to Earth.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-webb-pillars-2022">The Pillars of Creation are set within the vast Eagle Nebula, 6,500 light-years away.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-m16">For all their fame, the pillars are small compared to the whole nebula.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">The Cosmic Cliffs, in NGC 3324, are roughly 7,600 light-years away.</Claim>

## What nobody knows yet

<Unknowns>

- **How many stars a cloud will make, and how heavy they will be.** <Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">"What determines the number of stars that form in a certain region? Why do stars form with a certain mass?" are among the great open questions Webb is meant to address.</Claim>
- **Whether young stars mostly spark new ones or stop them.** <Claim source="nasa-webb-cosmic-cliffs">An eroding rim can trigger collapse or strip the material away, and Webb is expected to reveal the impact of star formation on the evolution of the gigantic clouds it happens in.</Claim>
- **How many stars are being born.** <Claim source="nasa-webb-pillars-2022">Webb's view of the Pillars will help researchers revamp their models of star formation by giving far more precise counts of newly formed stars.</Claim>
- **What drew the Ring Nebula's arcs.** <Claim source="esawebb-ring-nebula">Roughly ten concentric arcs lie just beyond the outer edge of the main ring. They are thought to come from the central star's interaction with a low-mass companion.</Claim>
