## Not a hole

The name misleads. <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">A black hole is not really a hole. It is a huge concentration of matter packed into a very tiny space.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">It is so dense that, just beneath its boundary, gravity is strong enough that nothing can escape, not even light.</Claim>

That boundary is called the event horizon. <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Inside it, the speed needed to get away is greater than the speed of light, which is as fast as anything can go. So whatever crosses the event horizon stays inside.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Because light cannot get out, a black hole neither gives off light nor reflects it, and nothing about what happens inside can reach an observer outside.</Claim>

Black holes are not cosmic vacuum cleaners either. <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">They do not suck in other matter: from far enough away, their gravity acts just like that of any other object of the same mass.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">If the Sun were replaced by a black hole of the same mass, the solar system would get a lot colder, but the planets would stay in their orbits.</Claim>

## Reading the picture

The scene at the top of this page is an illustration, but each thing in it stands for something real.

<Diagram name="black-hole-anatomy" source="nasa-anatomy nasa-types">

1. **Event horizon.** The boundary itself, the part that makes a black hole black.
2. **Shadow.** The dark zone we actually see. The horizon captures light and the warped space around it redirects light, so the dark zone is roughly twice as big as the hole's surface.
3. **Photon ring.** Thin rings of light at the edge of the shadow. Light from the disc circles the hole, sometimes several times, before it escapes toward us.
4. **Accretion disc.** Gas that has settled into a hot, bright, rapidly spinning disc. It is the main source of light.
5. **Jets.** A little of the material heading for the hole may be rerouted into a pair of jets that blast away in opposite directions.
6. **Singularity.** What general relativity predicts at the very centre. See the note below.

</Diagram>

Two things in the picture look wrong and are not. <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">The hole's gravity warps space-time, and light has to follow the distorted path. Astronomers call this gravitational lensing.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Light from the top of the disc behind the hole appears as a hump above it, and light from beneath the far side makes another hump below.</Claim> That is why the disc seems to wrap over the shadow.

One side of the disc is also brighter than the other. <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Near the hole the disc spins so fast that light from the part turning toward us becomes brighter and bluer, while light from the part turning away becomes dimmer and redder.</Claim>

<Evidence status="theoretical">

<Claim source="nasa-anatomy">General relativity predicts that the very centre of a black hole contains a point where matter is crushed to infinite density: the singularity.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">It may be a physical structure or a purely mathematical one, and right now astronomers do not know which.</Claim> That is why the singularity is drawn here in dashed ink.

</Evidence>

## How one forms

<Claim source="nasa-black-holes">One type of black hole is born when massive stars run out of fuel and explode in supernovae.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-types">When such a star runs out of fuel its core collapses, rebounds and explodes. If the star had around 20 times the Sun's mass or more, the core collapses into a stellar-mass black hole.</Claim>

A black hole can keep growing after that. <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Black holes grow by consuming matter, a process scientists call accretion, and by merging with other black holes.</Claim>

## How big they get

<Claim source="nasa-types">Astronomers generally sort black holes into three groups by mass: stellar-mass, supermassive and intermediate-mass. The boundaries between the groups are approximate.</Claim>

- **Stellar-mass.** <Claim source="nasa-types">These range from a few to hundreds of times the Sun's mass.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-types">Scientists think there may be as many as 100 million of them in our galaxy alone.</Claim>
- **Supermassive.** <Claim source="nasa-types">Almost every large galaxy, including our Milky Way, has one at its centre, with hundreds of thousands to billions of times the Sun's mass.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">Ours is called Sagittarius A*, and it has 4 million times the Sun's mass.</Claim>
- **Intermediate-mass.** <Claim source="nasa-types">These would fill the gap between the other two. Numerous candidates have been identified, but they have proven difficult to confirm.</Claim>

The record holders are far apart. <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">The lightest known black hole has only 3.8 times the Sun's mass. The most massive one observed, TON 618, has 66 billion times the Sun's mass.</Claim>

<Evidence status="hypothetical">

There may be a fourth kind. <Claim source="nasa-types">Scientists theorise that primordial black holes formed in the first second after the birth of the universe, when pockets of hot material may have been dense enough to collapse.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-types">Scientists have not yet found definitive proof that they ever existed.</Claim>

</Evidence>

## Too close

<Claim source="nasa-too-close">An object that comes too close to a black hole feels tidal forces: gravity pulls differently on its near side and its far side, and that distorts it.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">The result has a real name, spaghettification. The matter is squeezed horizontally and stretched vertically, like a noodle.</Claim>

Time is affected too. <Claim source="nasa-too-close">Near a black hole time passes more slowly than it does farther away, an effect called time dilation.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-too-close">We experience a very small amount of it on Earth, because of Earth's own gravity.</Claim>

## How we know they are there

A thing that gives off no light has to be found by what it does to its surroundings. <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">Scientists detect and study black holes mainly through their effects on what is around them.</Claim>

**The glow of gas.** <Claim source="nasa-types">In some pairs of a black hole and a star, the hole pulls gas off the star into a disc that heats up enough to produce X-rays.</Claim> <Claim source="chandra-cygnus-x1">About a decade after the X-ray source Cygnus X-1 was discovered, X-ray and optical observations together led to the conclusion that it was a black hole, the first such identification.</Claim>

**The orbits of stars.** <Claim source="nasa-black-holes">Astronomers tracked the orbits of several stars near the centre of the Milky Way to prove that it houses a supermassive black hole, a discovery that won the 2020 Nobel Prize.</Claim>

**Ripples in space-time.** <Claim source="ligo-gw150914">On 14 September 2015 both LIGO detectors recorded gravitational waves produced in the final fraction of a second of the merger of two black holes. A collision like that had been predicted but never observed.</Claim> <Claim source="ligo-gw150914">The two black holes were estimated at about 29 and 36 times the mass of the Sun, and the event took place 1.3 billion years ago.</Claim>

**A picture.** <Claim source="eso-m87-image">In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope, which links telescopes around the globe into an Earth-sized virtual telescope, revealed the black hole at the centre of the galaxy Messier 87: a ring-like structure with a dark central region, the black hole's shadow.</Claim> <Claim source="eso-m87-image">That black hole is 55 million light-years from Earth and has a mass 6.5 billion times that of the Sun.</Claim> <Claim source="eso-sgr-a-image">In 2022 the same collaboration showed the one at the centre of our own galaxy, about 27,000 light-years from Earth: again a dark shadow surrounded by a bright ring.</Claim>

## What nobody knows yet

<Unknowns>

- **What is at the centre.** <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">The prediction of a singularity may signal the limits of relativity.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Nothing that happens inside the horizon can reach an outside observer, so it cannot be checked directly.</Claim>
- **How the giants grew.** <Claim source="nasa-types">Scientists are not sure how supermassive black holes came to be. Observations of distant galaxies show that some formed in the first billion years after the birth of the universe.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-origins">How they got to be so huge so fast is one of the outstanding mysteries in astronomy.</Claim>
- **Where the middle sizes are.** <Claim source="nasa-types">Scientists are puzzled by the size gap between stellar-mass and supermassive black holes, and are actively hunting for examples in between.</Claim>
- **How jets work.** <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Jets fire out particles at close to the speed of light, but astronomers do not fully understand how.</Claim>

</Unknowns>
