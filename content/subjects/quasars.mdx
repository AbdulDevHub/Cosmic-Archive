## A light brighter than a galaxy

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Picture a region about the size of the solar system pouring out 100 to 1,000 times as much light as an entire galaxy of a hundred billion stars.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">These objects are quasars: distant galaxies whose incredibly bright cores are powered by supermassive black holes.</Claim>

<Claim source="esahubble-quasar">Quasars are among the most luminous objects in the known universe.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-quasars">A quasar's light outshines all the stars in its host galaxy combined.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Today quasars are counted as one kind of active galactic nucleus: a galaxy with an extremely bright core, lit by matter falling into a central supermassive black hole. Quasars are the most powerful kind.</Claim> <Claim source="esahubble-quasar">All quasars are active galactic nuclei, but not all active galactic nuclei are quasars.</Claim>

## Reading the picture

The picture at the top of this page is our own illustration of a quasar seen from very far off. It is the black hole from the Black Holes page, pulled back until its disc is a small bright oval. Each labelled part stands for something seen in real ones.

At the centre is the core. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Gas and dust spiral around the black hole in a disc before they fall in. Gravity and friction heat this accretion disc to millions of degrees and make it shine.</Claim>

Out of the disc's two faces run the jets. <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">A little of the material heading for a black hole can be rerouted into a pair of jets that blast away in opposite directions, firing out particles at close to the speed of light.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">When a pair of jets is angled toward us, the one firing our way looks much brighter and the one firing away much dimmer. The effect is called Doppler beaming.</Claim> That is why one jet in the picture is fainter than the other.

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">Strong jets can travel far out into intergalactic space, the space between the galaxies.</Claim> In the picture each jet ends in a wide, faint lobe.

Around it all is the host galaxy, drawn faint on purpose. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Together, the jets and the glowing disc outshine the galaxy they live in.</Claim>

The jets are drawn side on, so that both can be seen; the diagram below shows why a real quasar is thought to be seen from nearer its jet, and not every quasar has jets at all.

The line along the jets, with a bar at each end, is this site's ink: a surveyor's line across the whole span. The colours are this site's palette, not the colours of real light.

## How a quasar shines

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">In a normal galaxy the light is roughly the sum of its stars. An active galaxy gives off a great deal more than that, in infrared, radio, ultraviolet and X-rays.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">That extra light comes from a small, very bright core, which often changes in brightness.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">The supermassive black hole at the centre is pulling in material from the galaxy's dense middle. As it falls, the material spirals in and forms a disc, which heats up through gravity and friction.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-quasars">As material spirals into the black hole, a large part of its mass is turned into energy, and that is the light we see.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">Only about one in ten active galactic nuclei produces jets.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-quasars">Most quasars give off very little radio light, though the name, short for "quasi-stellar radio source", has been kept anyway.</Claim>

<Evidence status="theoretical">

<Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">Models of active galaxies also include a ring of cold gas and dust, thought to be shaped like a giant donut, with the black hole and its disc in the hole of the donut.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-agn">Some of the differences between kinds of active galactic nuclei come from the angle we see them at. With quasars and blazars, we are looking down the jet.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-imagine-quasars">Blazars are an extreme case, where we may be looking straight into the jet.</Claim> The donut is drawn below in long-dashed ink because it is a model.

</Evidence>

<Diagram name="quasar-views" source="nasa-imagine-agn nasa-imagine-quasars">

1. **Black hole and accretion disc.** The disc of infalling gas, hottest near its inner edge. It is the light at the heart of a quasar.
2. **Donut of gas and dust.** Cold material around the disc, as models of active galaxies have it.
3. **Jets.** Beams of particles thrown out in opposite directions, away from the disc.
4. **Straight down the jet.** Seen this way, the core is a blazar.
5. **Close to the jet.** Seen this way, it is a quasar.

</Diagram>

## Black holes and galaxies

A quasar is where [Black Holes](/subjects/black-holes) and [Galaxies](/subjects/galaxies) meet.

<Claim source="nasa-galaxies">Most large galaxies have a supermassive black hole at their centre, some with billions of times the Sun's mass.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-types">These black holes have hundreds of thousands to billions of times the Sun's mass. Ours, Sagittarius A*, is 4 million times the mass of the Sun.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">A quasar happens when immense amounts of matter fall into one of these black holes.</Claim>

The galaxy feeds the hole. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">Close to the quasar 3C 273, Hubble found blobs and a mysterious L-shaped filament, all within 16,000 light-years of the black hole. Some may be small satellite galaxies falling in, bringing the material that will power it.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">Earlier Hubble images suggested that galaxies colliding and merging with quasars send debris cascading down onto their black holes, which reignites them.</Claim>

<Evidence status="theoretical">

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Because quasars need so much matter, astronomers think they start when galaxies merge, which happened much more often in the early universe.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">But Hubble also found quasars in galaxies that look undisturbed, which may mean a subtler way of feeding a black hole.</Claim>

</Evidence>

And the hole changes the galaxy. <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Hubble found that the push of radiation near a quasar's black hole drives material out of the galaxy's centre at a fraction of the speed of light, hundreds of times the Sun's mass of it each year.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-webb-quasars">Gas is the fuel for new stars, so when outflows carry it away, fewer stars form. Some outflows are strong enough to stop star formation in the galaxy altogether.</Claim>

The two grow together. <Claim source="esawebb-canucs">Astronomers have seen that a supermassive black hole and its galaxy are linked: the larger the galaxy grows, the larger its central black hole becomes.</Claim>

## The first quasar

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">Quasars were first found in the 1950s and 1960s and called "quasi-stellar radio sources", because they looked like stars but gave off radio waves.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">The quasar 3C 273 was identified in 1963 by the astronomer Maarten Schmidt as the first quasar. At 2.5 billion light-years away it was too far off to be a star, so it had to be more energetic than anyone had imagined.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">The likely source of that energy was material falling onto a black hole.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">3C 273 is still one of the closest quasars to Earth. Its jet is 300,000 light-years long and moves across space at nearly the speed of light.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">If the quasar were only a few tens of light-years away, it would look as bright as the Sun.</Claim>

## How far, how many

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">The closest quasars are hundreds of millions of light-years away. Light takes so long to cross that distance that we see them as they were in the earlier universe.</Claim> <Claim source="esahubble-quasar">That there are none nearer does not mean our part of the universe never had quasars. It means quasars belong to the time when the universe was younger.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-hubble-3c273">At least a million quasars are scattered across the sky. They were most common about 3 billion years after the big bang, when galaxies collided more often.</Claim>

<Claim source="nasa-webb-quasars">Their light makes them useful far beyond themselves: astronomers use quasars as bright lamps behind the gas between galaxies, to see what that gas is made of.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-quasars">In 2019 Hubble found the brightest quasar known in the early universe, with the help of a galaxy in front of it whose gravity magnified its light. It shines like about 600 trillion Suns.</Claim>

## What nobody knows yet

- **How the jets work.** <Claim source="nasa-anatomy">Jets fire particles out at close to the speed of light, but astronomers do not fully understand how they work.</Claim>
- **How the black holes grew so big so fast.** <Claim source="nasa-hubble-origins">How supermassive black holes got so huge so quickly is one of the outstanding mysteries in astronomy.</Claim> <Claim source="nasa-hubble-origins">Current theories predict they begin in the dust-shrouded cores of galaxies that are forming stars furiously, then blow the gas and dust away and emerge as quasars.</Claim>
- **Which came first, the galaxy or the black hole.** <Claim source="esawebb-qso1">In the tiny early galaxy Abell2744-QSO1, Webb measured a black hole of about 50 million times the Sun's mass that makes up two-thirds of the galaxy's mass. It may have formed before its galaxy did.</Claim> <Claim source="esawebb-canucs">In another early galaxy, CANUCS-LRD-z8.6, the black hole is more massive than the usual link with its galaxy would allow.</Claim>
- **Whether our galaxy was once a quasar.** <Claim source="nasa-imagine-quasars">The Milky Way has a supermassive black hole at its centre, but whether it was ever a quasar is still debated: it is possible, and there is no proof either way.</Claim>
